Why it matters

Without a will, the law decides who gets what.

If you die without a will in England and Wales, your estate is shared out under the rules of intestacy. Those rules follow a fixed order of family members and take no account of what you would have wanted.

  • Unmarried partners have no automatic right to inherit, however long you have lived together.
  • A will lets you name guardians for children under 18, so the people you trust raise them.
  • A Lasting Power of Attorney means the people you choose can help if you become unwell.

Wills, LPAs and trusts

Every family is different. We take the time to understand yours, then recommend only what you need.

01

Wills

A will is the foundation of any estate plan. It sets out who receives your money, property and possessions when you die, and who you trust to deal with it all.

  • Choose who inherits and in what shares
  • Appoint executors to carry out your wishes
  • Name guardians for children under 18
  • Leave specific gifts, such as jewellery or a sum of money
  • Record your funeral wishes

We recommend reviewing your will after any big life event, such as marriage, divorce, a new baby or moving home. In England and Wales, getting married cancels any will you made before.

02

Mirror wills

Mirror wills are two separate wills for a couple that follow the same pattern. Usually each of you leaves everything to the other, and then to the same people, often your children, after you have both passed away.

  • A cost effective option for married couples, civil partners and couples who live together
  • Each will is still your own and can be changed by you later
  • Can include trusts to protect your share of the family home
03

Lasting Powers of Attorney

A Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA) lets you choose the people who can make decisions for you if illness or an accident means you can no longer make them yourself. Without one, your family may have to apply to the Court of Protection, which can be slow and costly.

There are two types, and many people choose both:

  • Property and Financial Affairs covers paying bills, managing bank accounts and selling property
  • Health and Welfare covers medical treatment, care and where you live

An LPA can only be made while you still have mental capacity, and it must be registered with the Office of the Public Guardian before it can be used. We handle the paperwork and registration for you.

04

Trusts

A trust lets you set aside money or property to be looked after by people you choose (trustees) for the benefit of others (beneficiaries). Trusts give you more control over what happens to your assets and when.

  • Property protection trusts help make sure your share of the home passes to your children, even if your partner remarries
  • Discretionary trusts give your trustees flexibility to support beneficiaries as their needs change
  • Life insurance in trust can mean a payout reaches your family faster and sits outside your estate

Trusts can be complex, so we always explain how they work in your situation before you decide.

🌱 FAQs

Your questions answered

Can't see your question here? Give us a call and we will be happy to help.

I'm married. Do I still need a will?

Yes. Without a will, your husband, wife or civil partner may not inherit everything, especially if you have children. A will makes sure your estate is shared exactly as you want.

My partner and I aren't married. What happens if one of us dies?

Unmarried partners have no automatic right to inherit under the intestacy rules, however long you have been together. Making wills is the simplest way to protect each other.

What is the difference between a will and a Lasting Power of Attorney?

A will only takes effect after you die. A Lasting Power of Attorney covers your lifetime, letting people you trust make decisions if you are unable to make them yourself. Most people benefit from having both.

When should I make a Lasting Power of Attorney?

While you are fit and well. An LPA can only be made while you have the mental capacity to do so. If you wait until it is needed, it may be too late.

How often should I review my will?

We suggest every three to five years, and always after a big life event such as marriage, divorce, the birth of a child or grandchild, or buying a new home. Remember that getting married cancels any existing will in England and Wales.

Do I need a trust?

Not everyone does. Trusts can be useful if you want to protect your home for your children, provide for someone vulnerable, or make sure a life insurance payout reaches your family quickly. We will tell you honestly whether a trust would help you.
