Do I need a will if I'm not married?

Updated November 2026

If you live with your partner but you're not married or in a civil partnership, a will is one of the most important documents you can make. Here's why.

The short answer: yes

In England and Wales, unmarried partners have no automatic right to inherit from each other. It doesn't matter how long you've lived together, whether you have children, or whether people call you a "common law" couple. Common law marriage doesn't exist in law.

If you die without a will, your estate is shared out under the intestacy rules. Those rules only recognise husbands, wives, civil partners and blood relatives. Your partner isn't on the list.

What happens to your home

It depends on how you own it.

  • Joint tenants: your share passes automatically to your partner when you die, whether you have a will or not.
  • Tenants in common: you each own a set share. Without a will, your share goes to your relatives under the intestacy rules, which could be your children, your parents or your brothers and sisters. Your partner could end up sharing the home with them, or even having to sell.
  • In your sole name: your partner has no automatic right to stay in the home at all.

If you're not sure how your home is owned, you can check the title with HM Land Registry, or we can help you find out.

Pensions and life insurance

Many pensions and life insurance policies can pay out to a person you name, separately from your will. Check that you've filled in an expression of wish or nomination form for your pension, and ask whether your life insurance is written in trust. These are easy to overlook and can make a big difference to your partner.

Can my partner make a claim?

An unmarried partner who lived with you as a couple for at least two years before your death can apply to the court for "reasonable financial provision" under the Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975. There are strict time limits, usually six months from the grant of probate or letters of administration. Claims like this can be slow, stressful and expensive, and there's no guarantee of success. A will avoids putting your partner in that position.

If you have children

A will is the only way to name the guardians you'd want to look after children under 18 if both parents die. You can also decide when your children should inherit, rather than leaving it to the law.

If you're planning to marry

In England and Wales, getting married or forming a civil partnership cancels any will you made before, unless the will was written with that marriage in mind. If you're engaged, tell us so we can write your will to survive the wedding.

What we recommend

  • Make wills that leave what you want to each other. Mirror wills are often the simplest option for couples.
  • Check how your home is owned and whether that still suits you.
  • Consider a property protection trust if you want to make sure your share of the home eventually passes to your children.
  • Review your pension nominations and life insurance.
