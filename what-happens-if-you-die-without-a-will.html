What happens if you die without a will in England and Wales?

Updated November 2026

When someone dies without a valid will, the law decides who inherits. These are called the rules of intestacy, and they often don't match what people would have wanted.

Who inherits under the intestacy rules

The rules follow a fixed order, set out in the Administration of Estates Act 1925.

  • Married or in a civil partnership, with no children: your husband, wife or civil partner inherits everything.
  • Married or in a civil partnership, with children: your spouse or civil partner receives your personal possessions, the first £322,000 of your estate (for deaths on or after 26 July 2023), and half of anything above that. Your children share the other half equally.
  • Not married, with children: your children share everything equally.
  • No spouse and no children: your estate passes to your parents, then if they've died, to your brothers and sisters, and then to more distant relatives such as half brothers and sisters, grandparents, and aunts and uncles.
  • No living relatives who qualify: your estate goes to the Crown.

A spouse or civil partner must survive you by 28 days to inherit under these rules. Children under 18 can't receive their share directly, so it's held for them until they turn 18.

Who misses out

  • Unmarried partners, however long you've been together.
  • Stepchildren, unless you legally adopted them.
  • Friends, carers and charities, however close or important they were to you.

A husband or wife you've separated from but not divorced is still treated as your spouse and can still inherit.

Who deals with your estate

Without a will, nobody has been chosen to sort out your affairs. A close relative has to apply for "letters of administration" before they can deal with your money and property. This can add delay and stress at an already difficult time, especially if family members disagree about who should do it.

Children and guardians

If you have children under 18 and no will, you haven't named a guardian. If there's no surviving parent with parental responsibility, the court may have to decide who looks after them.

How to make sure your wishes are followed

A will lets you decide who inherits, who looks after your children, and who you trust to carry out your wishes. It can also include trusts to protect your home or a family member who needs extra support. For most people, making a will is straightforward, and we'll explain each step in plain English.
